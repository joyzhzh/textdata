# C003 analytical account — expanded sample

Provisional analysis of 22 interviews over three cycles, with six current categories. Status: NON_RELEASE; saturation not reached. This public account preserves the interpretations and limits; internal handoff instructions are omitted.

The second wave supports a narrower and more useful question than 'does AI give people agency?': **whose purposes govern the work when greater capacity encounters an unwelcome consequence, and who can obtain a consequential change?** The analysis distinguishes (1) producing an acceptable result, (2) being able to authorize and redirect the process, and (3) having discretion over the commitments that result. These are proposed analytical distinctions, not validated measurements or universal necessary conditions.

Shipper's investor email is the strongest contrary case to equating checkpoint compliance with useful output. He reports that Codex skipped expected approval, yet the email was exactly what he would have sent. The local result was acceptable; the procedure breached his expectation. It does not show what happens when output is unwelcome, whether the recipient reacted favorably, or whether future sends became controllable. A theory predicting every unreviewed output is harmful fails this case. A theory concerned with counterfactual capacity to redirect must acknowledge that capacity is not directly observed here.

The Proof account sharpens the difference between more patches and a changed diagnosis. Shipper reports internal/beta tests, launch failures, patch cascades, then two senior-engineer rewrites; the engineers themselves used AI. He attributes their different approach to understanding and time that he lacked. The existence of rewrites is reported, but durable production recovery is not demonstrated. This supports investigation of reframing as a response, not a proven cure. His paused hosted OpenClaw product supplies an additional resource-rich limit: access to engineers and tools does not guarantee repair when the underlying harness changes rapidly. Sander’s claimed prompt improvement supplies a reported local change; he is a prompting specialist and obtained coded examples, so it does not demonstrate minimally supported or nonexpert repair.

Elena Verna reports building a first site herself, then recruiting Lazar because her desired initiatives exceeded available time. Depending on another builder can be chosen capacity allocation, rather than evidence that she cannot act. The same speaker reports stopping ideas after prototyping and sometimes refusing meetings to protect family time. Her use of AI is therefore compatible with declining projects and commitments. These are a senior leader's accounts; ordinary employees' bargaining position and consequences of refusal remain unobserved. Her prototype stopping can reflect clearer knowledge of an idea or accommodation to a tool's limits; the account does not distinguish them. Her links to Lazar mean the two interviews illuminate a connected setting, not independent corroborations.

Sander's privacy sequence resists a performance-maximizing definition of repair. Removing an identifying email and anonymizing names reportedly lowered classification performance. The transcript does not disclose the final published prompt or whether privacy ultimately prevailed. It establishes a conflict among criteria and attempted revisions, not a resolved ethical trade-off. Medical and psychological claims surrounding the research task are not used as evidence. Nick Turley's account similarly locates criteria beyond a local user: a provider reports a release that over-affirmed people, then new measurement and an explicit statement of goals. No rollback or independently verified improvement is established. A design intended to make users feel in control is distinct from evidence that they can interrupt or reverse consequential action.

The organizational accounts test how increased capacity is translated into expectations. Managerial AI feedback, employee self-coaching, agent-guided planning and more contributions can change who evaluates whom and who absorbs coordination work. These are possible redistributions of judgment and obligation; neither employee harm nor employee empowerment follows automatically. A manager's reported invitation to challenge leadership is a contrary account to a claim of universal silencing, but a debate does not demonstrate changed decisions. A founder's reported fixed rating formula, dismissals, dissent and subsequent survivor approval cannot establish that AI caused turnover or that workers endorsed the change. Broader turnaround strategy and pre-existing hierarchy remain live explanations.

Wu’s experiment motivates a possible context-based intervention route without manual takeover, but has no completed repair outcome. It cannot refute a necessary condition for successful intervention; the condition remains open.

Noam Segal relays survey concerns about workload alongside excitement, enjoyment and reported usefulness. He explicitly describes feelings, and notes selection among active founders. These mediated aggregate accounts cannot verify prevalence, within-person trajectories, cognitive decline or causal effects. They do make a uniformly celebratory reading of elite success accounts less tenable. Dhanji's explicit attribution of organizational change to a functional reorganization rather than AI must be retained as a rival, not absorbed into an AI-only explanation.

The emerging process has alternative paths rather than a mandatory ladder: a person or organization takes up an affordance; output or use changes what seems possible; a discrepancy becomes visible under particular criteria; responses may repair locally, recruit a different diagnosis, change infrastructure, narrow scope, postpone or stop. Outcomes can be acceptable without authorized procedure; authorized attempts can fail; new capability can increase new demands. Whose criteria and commitments prevail may depend on organizational relations; this is a candidate explanation, not an established causal relationship.

To avoid circularity, revision capacity must not be inferred from success. Future research should record access, permissions, time and expertise before a failure, then observe an attempted redirection and its consequences. It should retain well-resourced failure and unsupported success, and distinguish repair of a system from preserving the person's purpose. The next indispensable contrast is matched work with different authority/resource positions and direct worker accounts, including actual refusal or disagreement through known outcomes. Archive additions have changed the theory; recurrence has not established saturation.

## Stop reason and unresolved discriminators

This experimental expansion ends after the two adaptive archive waves have been coded, compared and reviewed for the stated contrasts. It is a completed analysis of the selected interviews, not completion of all 344 interviews or a claim that further archival analysis could add nothing. The second wave changed categories and introduced additional contrary cases; it did not establish saturation. The remaining gaps are specified in REQ003: direct ordinary-worker disagreement/refusal through a known consequence, durable repair after specialist or provider intervention, comparable tasks with pre-existing resources/authority measured, and adjudication of changed goals versus rationalization.

The screens and selected interviews did not supply those contrasts adequately. Their absence from this run does not prove absence from the archive. Further leader accounts alone would not substitute for the missing worker histories. External primary traces, participants or survey data were not acquired, and no contacts were made. Future acquisition requires a separate scope; the stored request remains PROPOSED. Four memo types and versioned negative-case responses remain operative. The stop status is NOT_REACHED and release status NON_RELEASE.
